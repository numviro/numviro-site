PAYCHECK BASICS

Gross Pay vs Net Pay

Gross pay is what you earn before payroll deductions. Net pay is what remains after taxes, benefits, and other deductions are taken out — the amount commonly called take-home pay.

Simple formula Gross Pay − Deductions = Net Pay

Your gross pay starts the paycheck. Your net pay is what is left after payroll deductions.

The exact path from gross to net can be more detailed because some deductions may be taken before certain taxes and others after taxes. Your pay stub shows the actual earnings, taxes, and deductions used by your employer.

GROSS PAY

What is gross pay?

Gross pay is the total amount you earn before payroll deductions are removed. For an hourly employee, gross pay can include regular wages, overtime, bonuses, commissions, tips, or other earnings that apply to the pay period.

Hourly Gross Pay = Hourly Rate × Hours Worked + Other Earnings

For a salaried employee, the gross amount for one paycheck is commonly based on annual salary divided by the number of pay periods, plus any additional earnings that apply.

Want to calculate earnings before deductions? Use the Gross Pay Calculator.

NET PAY

What is net pay?

Net pay is the amount left after payroll deductions are subtracted from gross pay. It is commonly called take-home pay because it is the amount the employee actually receives.

Your net pay can change even when your gross pay stays the same. Changes to tax withholding, insurance, retirement contributions, or other deductions can change the amount that reaches your bank account.

EXAMPLE

Gross pay vs net pay example

Suppose an employee has $2,500 in gross pay for a pay period. If the paycheck has $650 in total taxes, benefits, and other deductions:

$2,500 Gross Pay − $650 Deductions = $1,850 Net Pay

The employee earned $2,500 before deductions but receives $1,850 after those deductions. The $650 difference is not one single tax — it represents the combined deductions in this example.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

Gross pay is not the same as taxable income

Gross pay, taxable wages, taxable income, and net pay are related but they are not always the same number. Certain payroll deductions can affect taxable wages, while income-tax calculations can involve additional rules outside the paycheck itself.

For everyday paycheck reading, the simplest distinction is:

Gross = earned before deductions   |   Net = received after deductions

WHEN EACH NUMBER MATTERS

Should you look at gross pay or net pay?

Use gross pay when comparing salary offers, hourly rates, or total earnings before deductions.

Use net pay when planning rent, bills, savings, and other spending because net pay is closer to the cash actually available from the paycheck.

A job offer may advertise gross salary, while your household budget usually needs the expected net paycheck.

FAQ

Gross pay vs net pay FAQ

What is the difference between gross pay and net pay?

Gross pay is earnings before payroll deductions. Net pay is the amount remaining after taxes, benefits, and other payroll deductions are taken out.

Is net pay the same as take-home pay?

Yes. Net pay is commonly called take-home pay because it is the amount an employee receives after payroll deductions.

Is gross pay before or after taxes?

Gross pay is before payroll taxes and deductions are subtracted.

Why is my net pay lower than my gross pay?

Net pay is lower because payroll taxes, benefit contributions, and other applicable deductions are subtracted from gross pay.

Can two people with the same gross salary have different net pay?

Yes. Net pay can differ because tax withholding, benefits, retirement contributions, and other deductions can vary between employees.

How do I calculate gross pay?

For hourly work, a basic gross-pay calculation starts with hourly rate multiplied by hours worked, then adds applicable overtime and other earnings. Numviro's Gross Pay Calculator can help with that calculation.

Does the estimator on this page calculate taxes for me?

No. The estimator only subtracts the tax and deduction amounts you enter. It does not calculate federal, state, local, or other tax liability.
